\subsection{Introduction}
{\parindent=0pt
In this paper we examine a notion of the size of a set called 
{\it fractal dimension} that enables one to more
finely distinguish the density of a given set than the usual
dimensional classification of $0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$.%  
\ftn{The $0$-dimensional volume of a finite set is its cardinality;
for an infinite set it is $+\infty$.}
Such a notion is motivated by sets that, while constructed in one of the
standard dimensions $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and robust in their structure,
nevertheless have zero ``volume'' in the ambient space containing them.
Such sets beg for a more nuanced ``size'' metric.

The sets that we are interested in are non-physical sets that
have the property that they retain structure no matter how much 
they are magnified. We call this class of sets
{\it fractal sets}, or
{\it fractals}. 

A commonplace example that resembles a fractal is the coastline of
a beach. If one were to take a map of the coastline and blow it up
again and again, one would find that it retains structure in the
sense that the expanded maps would not ``straighten out''.
Because this is a physical example, the complicated structure would fade
after a finite number of expansions. A true fractal, say representing a 
coastline, would retain complicated
structure no matter how many times a piece of it was magnified.
Because of this, the length of any section of this idealized coastline is
infinite. Another ``natural'' fractal is the surface of an idealized sponge.
While defined in a three dimensional world, one can construct such a sponge so that
it retains complicated structure no matter how many times it is magnified.
In addition, such a constructed sponge could have an infinite surface area and 
also have 0 volume. 

It would be convenient if there were a way to
measure the density of an idealized coastline to
indicate that it is, in some sense, larger than a one dimensional
object; or a way to show that the idealized surface of a sponge is larger than
two dimensions. This would allow us to compare the ``size'' of such sets
in a more precise way.

This is what the fractal dimension of a set tries to address. 
In the case of the coastline, it will give us a dimension between one and two
-- where the coastline ``naturally'' lives. The same holds for the surface of
the aforementioned sponge:
there will be a dimension between two and three where it naturally lives. In
each case, the fractal dimension is not an actual dimension, 
it is a way to quantify the density of such complex sets.
Yet, it should also ``agree'' with the usual dimensional size of an object 
if it is not a fractal. That is, the fractal dimension of a line segment should 
be 1; the fractal dimension of a filled triangle (its interior together with
its boundary) sitting in three dimensions should be 2, etc.

When studying any new phenomenon, it is natural to first examine the
simplest examples. The simplest such fractals are ones which have a
{\it self-similarity} property; namely, they have the property that if
one magnifies a piece of the fractal by a certain amount, the original
fractal is recovered. This regular structure makes them easier to
study mathematically than a general fractal. However, before we start
with more specific examples of fractals, we need to understand what
one might mean by fractal dimension.

Here is the plan. To measure the size of a set whose dimension we do
not yet know, we do what one always does with a set of unknown size:
cover it with small, simple sets; charge each covering set a price;
total the bill; and then refine the covers, taking a limit. This is
the same approximate-then-take-a-limit strategy that defines arc
length and the integral itself, so the reader already owns the move.
The only new ingredient is the {\it price\/}: what should an
$\alpha$-dimensional accounting charge for a covering set of diameter
$d$? In an integer dimension $n$ the natural charge is the volume of a
ball of radius $d/2$, which scales like $d^n$. So the first thing we
want is a formula for the volume of an $n$-dimensional ball in which
the dimension appears as a {\it parameter\/} -- one free to take
non-integer values. Producing that formula is the business of the next
section; the Gamma function is what lets the dimension come unstuck
from the integers.

\subsection{Volume of an n-Dimensional Sphere}
The goal of this section is the formula promised in the introduction:
the volume of an n-dimensional sphere, derived in a form where the
dimension appears as a parameter that need not be an integer.
The n-dimensional volume of any sphere is easy to compute if one
knows its surface area. Since the surface area of an n-dimensional sphere 
with radius, $r$, is $r^{n-1}$ times the surface of the unit sphere, 
it suffices to know the surface area of the unit sphere.
In what follows we will refer to an n-dimensional sphere of radius, $r$, 
as $B_n(r)$, and $B_n$ as the specific n-dimensional sphere of radius $1$.
We will use the notation, $S_n(r)$ and $V_n(r)$ for the surface area and volume 
of $B_n(r)$ respectively.

The relationship between $V_n(r)$ and $S_n(1)$ 
follows from the following formula:%

${V}_n(r) =  \int_{\partial B_n} \int_0^r \tau^{n-1} \,d\tau \,dS = \left(\int_{\partial B_n} \, dS\right) \left( \int_0^r \tau^{n-1} \, d\tau \right)$

The iterated integrals on the right hand side of the above equation can 
be easily computed, yielding: ${V}_n(r) = {S}_n(1) \frac{r^n}{n}$.

What remains now is to find a formula for $S_n(r)$.
This can be done by expressing an easily computable 
n-dimensional integral in $R^n$ as $n$ iterated integrals. 
And more importantly, this integral may be represented as a {\it different\/} 
spherically symmetric integral involving the surface area of $B_n$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2)} \, dx_1\, dx_2
\, \cdots \, dx_n = \int_{\partial B_n} \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r^{n-1} \,
dr\, dS
$$
The left side of the above can be written as an iterated integral of identical 
one dimensional integrals. The right side of the equation is the same 
integral but written in ``spherical coordinates'' which is a convenient choice as 
the integrand is spherically symmetric. The left side simplifies to 
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx$ raised to the $n^{\rm th}$ power;
which is $\pi^{n/2}$. Substituting $t = r^2$ in the inner integral on
the right hand side we have:

$$
\pi^{n/2} = \frac{1}{2} \int_{\partial B_n} \int_0^\infty 
e^{-t} t^{\frac{n}{2}-1} \,dt \, dS
$$
which may be written:%
\ftn{The function $\Gamma(z)$ is called the Gamma function. It is defined for
all real (and complex) arguments {\it except\/} the non-positive integers
$0, -1, -2, \ldots$, and satisfies $\Gamma(z+1) = z \Gamma(z)$ wherever
defined. On the positive integers it agrees with the factorial:
$\Gamma(n+1) = n!$ for $n \in Z^+$. In this sense $\Gamma$ extends the
factorial from the integers to the reals.}
$$
2 \pi^{n/2} = {S}_n(1) \Gamma(n/2)
$$
Or,
$$
{S}_n(1) = \frac{2 \pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}
$$
Consequently,%
\ftn{The surface area of an n-dimensional sphere scales by the radius to
the $(n-1)^{\rm th}$ power. This surface-area formula also does duty in
the companion paper ``Singular Transformations of Probability Density
Functions'', where it supplies the areas of the level-set spheres in a
coarea computation.}
$$
{S}_n(r) = \frac{2 \pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}r^{n-1}
$$
And so the volume of an n-dimensional sphere, $B_n(r)$, of radius $r$ is:%
$$
{V}_n(r) = \int_{B_n(r)} \, d\mu = \int_{\partial B_n} 
\int_0^r \tau^{n-1} \, d\tau \, dS = \left(\frac{r^n}{n}\right){S}_n(1)
$$
Which is
$$
{V}_n(r) = \frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{n \, \Gamma(n/2)} r^n
$$

\subsection{Fractal Dimension and Associated Volume of a Set}
We derived the formula for the n-dimensional volume of a sphere,
and the formula may take non-integral values. 
Armed with this formula, we make
the following definition.

\proclaim Definition. For any $\alpha \in R^+$, the $\alpha$-dimensional
volume, $V_\alpha$, of an n-dimensional sphere
of radius $r$ is defined to be:%
\ftn{The set $R^+$ is defined
by: $R^+ = \{x \, | \, x\in R \; \wedge \; x > 0\}$.}
$$
{V}_\alpha(r) = \frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\alpha \, \Gamma(\alpha/2)} r^\alpha
= \frac{\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2 + 1)} r^\alpha
$$
The second form uses the identity $\alpha \, \Gamma(\alpha/2) =
2 \, \Gamma(\alpha/2 + 1)$, and shows that -- despite appearances -- the
formula has no singularity as $\alpha \rightarrow 0^+$: the apparent poles
of $1/\alpha$ and $\Gamma(\alpha/2)$ cancel, and the limiting value is
$V_0(r) = 1$. This is consistent with the counting-measure convention that
the $0$-dimensional volume of a single point is $1$. We nevertheless
restrict to $\alpha > 0$ in what follows.

This is a mathematical construct, not a physical one; there is no claim
here of a physical fractional dimension. Also, note that the value is 
independent of the ambient space in which the ball might live, say $R^n$. 
That is, $\alpha$ may be taken to be larger or smaller than $n$.

Given that we have defined the fractional dimensional volume of a sphere 
in ``dimension'' $\alpha$ we wish to extend this to a definition of the 
fractional dimensional volume of {\it any\/} set.

\proclaim Definition. The $\alpha$-dimensional ($\alpha \in R^{+}$) {\it covering\/}
volume of a set $S \subseteq R^n$, ${\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon}(S)$,
with parameter $\epsilon \in R^+$, is defined as\ftn{The diameter of a
set is defined by:
{\rm Diam}(S) = $\sup\{|x - y| : x,y \in S\}$.}
$$
\eqalign{
	{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon}(S) & = \inf_{\{O_i\}_{i=1}^N}
    \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^N {V}_\alpha\left({\rm Diam}(O_i) / 2\right) \
    : S \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^N\limits O_i \; \wedge \; \left(\sup_{i \in [1,N]}
    {\rm  Diam}(O_i)\right) \le \epsilon \right\}
}
$$

Roughly speaking, for any given finite covering,
$\left\{ O_i \right\}_{i=1}^N$, and value $\epsilon$,
we estimate the $\alpha$-dimensional volume of the set, $S$, by charging
each covering set $O_i$ the $\alpha$-dimensional volume of a sphere of
radius ${\rm Diam}(O_i)/2$. That is, each summand may be written
$$
V_\alpha({\rm Diam}(O_i)/2) = c_\alpha \, {\rm Diam}(O_i)^\alpha,
\qquad c_\alpha = \frac{2^{1 - \alpha} \, \pi^{\alpha/2}}{\alpha \,
\Gamma(\alpha/2)}.
$$
The factor $c_\alpha$ is a normalization, chosen so that for integer
$\alpha$ the covering volume of ordinary solids agrees with their usual
volume. It is {\it not\/} literally the volume of the smallest sphere
containing $O_i$: a set of diameter $d$ need not fit inside a sphere of
radius $d/2$ (by Jung's theorem the smallest enclosing sphere in $R^n$ can
have radius as large as $d \sqrt{n/(2n + 2)}$).
The $\alpha$ covering volume of a set, $S$, is the infimum over all
such covers. The only restrictions on the covering sets are that they
be finite and that each have diameter no larger than $\epsilon$.

{\bf Remark.} The standard Hausdorff-measure definition differs from the
one above in one respect: it allows {\it countably infinite\/} covers, and
in general that is the definition one should use. For the compact sets
treated in this note the two definitions give the same answers, but they
can differ on non-closed or unbounded sets. For example, any finite cover
of the set of rationals in $[0,1]$ by sets of diameter at most $\epsilon$
has closures that cover all of $[0,1]$, so under finite covers the
rationals in $[0,1]$ receive dimension $1$; under countable covers -- each
rational covered by its own arbitrarily small interval -- they receive
dimension $0$. Likewise, an unbounded set admits no finite cover at all.
We keep finite covers here for concreteness, since our examples are
compact.

The intent of this definition is to give a practicable estimate of
the eventual $\alpha$ volume of a set, refined by forcing the covers to
become finer as $\epsilon$ decreases.

The covering volume is monotonically non-increasing in $\epsilon$:
$$
\eqalign{
	{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_1}(S) & \ge {\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_2}(S) \quad \quad \hbox{whenever  } \epsilon_1 \le \epsilon_2
}
$$
This follows as the set of coverings used to compute
${\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_1}(S)$ is a {\it subset\/} of those used to
compute ${\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_2}(S)$ (a tighter $\epsilon$ admits
fewer covers). Therefore, the infimum over the smaller family cannot be
smaller than the infimum over the larger family.

Using the covering volume, we may define the fractional dimensional volume by
taking a limit of increasingly refined covers.

\proclaim Definition. The $\alpha$-dimensional ($\alpha \in R^{+}$)
volume of a set $S \subseteq R^n$, ${\cal V}_\alpha(S)$,
is defined as\ftn{%
This definition is well defined, as the limit of a monotonic sequence exists,
provided we acknowledge that it may be infinite.}
$$
\eqalign{
	{\cal V}_\alpha(S) &= \lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0^+} {\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon}(S)
}
$$

While these statements define the $\alpha$ volume for any given set, $S$,
they do not tell us the ``natural'' fractal volume of $S$.
The following definition does precisely that.

\proclaim Definition. The {\bf fractal dimension} of a
set $S \subseteq R^m$ is defined as
$\alpha^* = \inf\{\alpha : {\cal V}_\alpha(S) = 0 \}$; the associated
{\bf fractal volume} of $S$ is defined to be ${\cal V}_{\alpha^*}(S)$.

That $\alpha^*$ is a genuine threshold -- with the $\alpha$ volume zero
above it and infinite below it -- is the content of the following
proposition.

\proclaim Proposition. Let $S \subseteq R^n$ and $0 < \alpha < \beta$. If
${\cal V}_\alpha(S) < \infty$, then ${\cal V}_\beta(S) = 0$. Equivalently,
if ${\cal V}_\beta(S) > 0$, then ${\cal V}_\alpha(S) = +\infty$.

{\bf Proof.} For any admissible cover, each set $O_i$ has
${\rm Diam}(O_i) \le \epsilon$, so
$$
\sum_{i=1}^N c_\beta \, {\rm Diam}(O_i)^\beta \le
\frac{c_\beta}{c_\alpha} \, \epsilon^{\beta - \alpha}
\sum_{i=1}^N c_\alpha \, {\rm Diam}(O_i)^\alpha
$$
Taking the infimum over covers gives
${\cal V}_{\beta, \epsilon}(S) \le (c_\beta / c_\alpha) \,
\epsilon^{\beta - \alpha} \, {\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon}(S) \le
(c_\beta / c_\alpha) \, \epsilon^{\beta - \alpha} \, {\cal V}_\alpha(S)$,
where the last step uses the monotonicity of ${\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon}$
in $\epsilon$. Letting $\epsilon \rightarrow 0^+$ yields
${\cal V}_\beta(S) = 0$.

Consequently, for $\alpha > \alpha^*$ the $\alpha$ volume of $S$ is zero,
and for $\alpha < \alpha^*$ it is infinite. At $\alpha = \alpha^*$ itself
the volume may be zero, a positive finite number, or infinite; when it is
positive and finite -- as in the examples below -- it serves as a natural
``size'' of $S$ in its natural dimension.

The quantity $\alpha^*$ is precisely the {\it Hausdorff dimension\/} of
$S$ (subject to the finite-cover caveat of the previous remark); see
Falconer [1] for the standard treatment. The term {\it fractal\/} was
coined by Mandelbrot [2].

Note that the fractal dimension of a set, $S$, does not depend on
the ambient space $S$ sits in.

\subsection{Computing the Fractal Dimension of a Simple Set}

The fractal dimension of a set $S$ should produce the usual
answer when $S$ is a non-fractal set. We show this for the case of a
straight line.

\example{Example}{A line segment in two dimensions.}
Let ${\bf x}, {\bf y} \in R^2$ and consider the line segment
$$
L = \{t{\bf x} + (1-t){\bf y} : t \in [0,1]\}.
$$
We wish to find the fractal dimension and fractal volume of $L$.
While this line segment sits in the two dimensional plane, we wish 
to find its ``natural'' dimension and associated volume.

For a given $n$, consider the sets $\{O_{n,i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}$, with $O_{n,i}~=~
[\frac{i}{n}{\bf x} + (1-\frac{i}{n}){\bf y}, \; \frac{i+1}{n}{\bf x} + (1 -
\frac{i+1}{n}){\bf y}]$ denoting the line segment in $R^2$ between the two
endpoints in brackets. The collection of line segments, $O_{n,i}$, act
as a cover for $L$; that is, $L =
\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}\limits O_{n,i}$. For a given $\alpha$ the
covering volume of $L$ with $\epsilon = \frac{1}{n} \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|$ 
is bounded above by
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} {\rm V}_\alpha({\rm Diam}(O_{n,i})/2) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
{\rm V}_\alpha(\|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|/(2n)) =  \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{2
\pi^{\alpha / 2}}{\alpha \Gamma(\alpha / 2)} \frac{\|{\bf x} - {\bf
y}\|^\alpha}{(2n)^\alpha} = \frac{2 \pi^{\alpha / 2} \|{\bf x} - {\bf
y}\|^\alpha}{2^\alpha \alpha \Gamma(\alpha / 2)} \frac{n}{n^\alpha}
$$

As $n$ increases, the covering becomes more refined and
the upper bound is dominated by the term
$\frac{n}{n^\alpha} = n^{1-\alpha}$. For $\alpha > 1$ this tends to zero
as $n \to \infty$, so ${\cal V}_\alpha(L) = 0$. For $\alpha = 1$ the bound
equals $c_1 \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|$ with $c_1 = \pi^{1/2}/\Gamma(1/2) = 1$,
so ${\cal V}_1(L) \le \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|$.

These covers give only {\it upper\/} bounds; to pin down the dimension we
need a matching lower bound valid for {\it every\/} cover. Let
$\{O_i\}_{i=1}^N$ be any cover of $L$ with $d_i = {\rm Diam}(O_i) \le
\epsilon$. Orthogonal projection onto the line through ${\bf x}$ and
${\bf y}$ does not increase diameters, and the projections of the $O_i$
cover the segment $L$, so
$$
\sum_{i=1}^N d_i \ge \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|.
$$
For $\alpha \le 1$ we have $d_i^\alpha = d_i^{\alpha - 1} d_i \ge
\epsilon^{\alpha - 1} d_i$ (as $d_i \le \epsilon$ and $\alpha - 1 \le 0$),
whence
$$
\sum_{i=1}^N V_\alpha(d_i / 2) = c_\alpha \sum_{i=1}^N d_i^\alpha
\ge c_\alpha \, \epsilon^{\alpha - 1} \, \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|.
$$
For $\alpha < 1$ the right side tends to $+\infty$ as
$\epsilon \rightarrow 0^+$, so ${\cal V}_\alpha(L) = +\infty$. For
$\alpha = 1$ it gives ${\cal V}_{1, \epsilon}(L) \ge \|{\bf x} - {\bf y}\|$,
which combined with the upper bound above yields
$$
{\cal V}_1(L) = \| {\bf x} - {\bf y}\|.
$$
Therefore, the fractal dimension of the line segment is
1 and its fractal volume is the length of the line segment, $\|{\bf x} - {\bf
y}\|$. This, of course, coincides with what we would previously regard
as the dimension and ``volume'' of a line segment.

Note that the answer would stay the same if the line segment was 
embedded in $R^n$ (for any $n \in Z^+$) rather than $R^2$.


This result is true for any ``ordinary'' set: its fractal dimension is its
``ordinary'' dimension. This means that we can think of fractal
dimension as a {\it generalization} of dimension. It is 
instructive to repeat the above analysis and compute the fractal dimension 
and fractal volume of a simple fractal.


\subsection{The Cantor Set: A Simple Fractal Set}

Perhaps the simplest self-similar fractal is the Cantor set, which is
formed by doing the following:
Take the unit interval, [0,1], and remove the middle third, (1/3,
2/3). This leaves the intervals, [0,1/3] and [2/3,1]. Now remove the
middle third of each of these two intervals, this gives four
intervals.
If we repeat this process indefinitely with the remaining
intervals, the resulting set is called the Cantor set, C. See 
the appendix for a graphical depiction of the construction process.
The Cantor
set has the property that $3 \cdot \left([0,1/3] \cap C\right) = C$; that is,
it is a self-similar fractal (where $\lambda \cdot S \equiv \{\lambda x : x \in S\}$).

The one dimensional length of the Cantor set is
easy to compute, it must be one (the length of the interval $[0,1]$) 
less the sum of all the lengths of the
intervals removed. This is computed as $1
- \sum_{n=1}^\infty (2^{n-1}/3^n) = 1 - \frac{1}{2}
\sum_{n=1}^\infty (2/3)^n = 0$. Since the Cantor set has zero length,
perhaps its zero dimensional ``length'' is a more natural description
of its size. But its zero dimensional length is equal to the 
number of points in the Cantor set; and, it turns out that this is infinite. 
What's more startling is
that, in a sense that can be made precise, there are as many points in
the Cantor set as there are in the interval $[0,1]$. So, although the 
Cantor set has no length, it is comparable in complexity
to $[0,1]$. This suggests that it might have a natural dimension
between zero and one.

\subsection{The Fractal Dimension of the Cantor Set and its Volume}
We compute the fractal dimension of the Cantor set. To do so, 
consider a refinement of coverings of the Cantor set, $C_n$, where
$C_n$ is the union of the set of $2^n$ intervals that remain after 
the $n^{th}$ stage of the Cantor construction process. Each of these 
intervals has length $(1/3)^n$. 
Clearly, $C \subseteq C_n$, and $C = \bigcap_{n=0}^\infty\limits C_n$
(with $C_0 = [0,1]$).

We label $I_{n,i}$ as the $i^{th}$ interval in the set
$C_n$. Each of these intervals has a diameter, $\epsilon_n = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^n$.
The collection, $\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^n}\limits I_{n,i}$, provides an
approximation to ${\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(C)$:


$$
{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(C) \le \sum_{i=1}^{2^n} {\rm V}_\alpha({\rm
  Diam}(I_{n,i}) / 2)
$$

As ${\rm Diam}(I_{n,i}) = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^n$, this becomes


$$
{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(C) \le \sum_{i=1}^{2^n} \frac{2
  \pi^{\alpha/2}}{ \alpha \Gamma(\alpha/2) 2^\alpha 3^{n\alpha}}
  =  \frac{
  2^{1 - \alpha} \pi^{\alpha/2}}{\alpha \Gamma(\alpha/2)} \;
  \sum_{i=1}^{2^n}
\frac{1}{3^{n\alpha}}
$$

Or, 
$$
{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(C) \le \frac{ 2^{1-\alpha} \pi^{\alpha/2}}{\alpha
  \Gamma(\alpha/2)} \left(\frac{2}{3^\alpha}\right)^n
$$


If $\frac{2}{3^\alpha} < 1$ then, in the limit as
$n \rightarrow \infty$,
this upper bound will be zero. This is the case
exactly when $\alpha > \ln 2 / \ln 3$; in which case
$$
{\cal V}_\alpha(C) =  \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} {\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(C) \le \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}  \frac{ 2^{1-\alpha}
\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\alpha
  \Gamma(\alpha/2)} \left(\frac{2}{3^\alpha}\right)^n = 0
$$
For $\alpha < \ln 2 / \ln 3$ the same expression diverges
($\frac{2}{3^\alpha} > 1$), so these covers no longer force the volume to
zero. In fact ${\cal V}_\alpha(C) = +\infty$ in that range: by the
Proposition, this follows from the positivity of the volume at the
threshold itself, ${\cal V}_{\alpha^*}(C) > 0$, discussed below.

The threshold value of $\alpha$ -- at which $\frac{2}{3^\alpha} = 1$ --
occurs when $\alpha^* = \ln 2 / \ln 3$. Therefore, the fractal dimension of the
Cantor set is $\alpha^* = \ln 2 / \ln 3 \approx 0.63093$.
Its fractal volume in this
dimension is bounded above by ${\cal V}_{\alpha^*}(C) \le \frac{2^{1-\alpha^*}
\pi^{\alpha^*/2}}{\alpha^* \Gamma(\alpha^* / 2)} = c_{\alpha^*} \approx 1.03505$.
A matching lower bound is genuinely harder: one must show that {\it no\/}
sequence of covers can do better than the natural covers used above. This
is a classical fact: in the standard normalization -- where a cover set of
diameter $d$ is charged the amount $d^\alpha$ rather than
$c_\alpha d^\alpha$ -- the $\alpha^*$-dimensional measure of the Cantor
set is exactly $1$; see Falconer [1], where the lower bound is obtained
by a mass distribution argument that exploits the self-similarity of $C$.
In our normalization this gives
$$
{\cal V}_{\alpha^*}(C) = c_{\alpha^*} \approx 1.03505.
$$

\subsection{A Shortcut for Self-Similar Fractals}
Looking back, the Cantor computation used only two numbers about the
Cantor set: at stage $n$ of the construction the natural cover
consisted of $N^n$ pieces (here $N = 2$), each a copy of the whole set
scaled by $r^n$ (here $r = 1/3$). That is worth replaying in general,
because it is {\it all\/} that self-similarity supplies. Suppose a
bounded set $S$, with $d = {\rm Diam}(S)$, is the union of $N$ copies
of itself, each scaled by a factor $r < 1$. Applying this description
to each copy, then to each copy of a copy, the $n^{\rm th}$ stage
presents $S$ as a union of $N^n$ copies of itself scaled by $r^n$.
These copies form a cover by sets of diameter $\epsilon_n = r^n d$, so
$$
{\cal V}_{\alpha, \epsilon_n}(S) \le \sum_{i=1}^{N^n}
c_\alpha \left(r^n d\right)^\alpha
= c_\alpha \, d^\alpha \left(N r^\alpha\right)^n
$$
Exactly as with the Cantor set, the fate of this bound as
$n \rightarrow \infty$ is decided by the factor $N r^\alpha$: the
bound collapses to zero when $N r^\alpha < 1$ and is useless when
$N r^\alpha > 1$. The threshold sits where $N r^\alpha = 1$; solving
for $\alpha$,
$$
\alpha^* = \frac{\ln N}{\ln (1/r)}
$$
For the Cantor set ($N = 2$, $r = 1/3$) the shortcut returns
$\ln 2 / \ln 3$ -- the answer of the previous section without a single
new integral.

The same honest caveat applies here as there: these covers force the
volume to zero {\it above\/} the threshold, so by themselves they show
only that the fractal dimension is {\it at most\/}
$\ln N / \ln(1/r)$. That it is not smaller requires a lower-bound
argument, such as the mass distribution argument cited earlier,
together with a condition that the $N$ copies do not overlap too much
(in the literature, the {\it open set condition\/}); for the standard
examples below both requirements hold, and the shortcut gives the true
dimension. See Falconer [1].

As with any new machine, the shortcut should first be calibrated
against sets whose dimension we already know. A line segment is two
copies of itself at scale $1/2$: dimension $\ln 2 / \ln 2 = 1$. A
filled triangle is four copies of itself at scale $1/2$ (the middle
copy inverted): dimension $\ln 4 / \ln 2 = 2$. The new machine
reproduces the answers the reader already owns -- which is how one
learns to trust a new tool. Now the interesting sets. The
Sierpi\'nski triangle of Appendix A keeps only three of the filled
triangle's four half-scale copies, and dropping the count from $4$ to
$3$ drops the dimension from $2$ to $\ln 3 / \ln 2 \approx 1.585$.
The idealized coastline of the introduction can be modeled by the
{\it Koch curve\/} -- four copies of itself at scale $1/3$ -- giving
$\ln 4 / \ln 3 \approx 1.262$. And the idealized sponge is the
{\it Menger sponge\/}: a cube is divided into $27$ third-scale
subcubes and the $20$ avoiding the center are kept, the process
repeating in each. Having no interior, the sponge is all surface, and
its dimension is $\ln 20 / \ln 3 \approx 2.727$.

The first two rows are the calibration; the remaining rows interpolate
between the integers -- which is precisely what fractal dimension was
invented to do.

\subsection{Conclusion}
We have shown that there are sets with sufficient complexity that the
usual categorization of dimension as a measure of size is lacking. 
We have further shown
that the notion of fractal dimension, as an extension to the usual
notion of dimension, provides a more discriminating view of the size
of a set.

The introduction promised numbers: a dimension between one and two
where an idealized coastline naturally lives, and one between two and
three for the surface of an idealized sponge. The machinery delivers
them. Modeling the coastline by the Koch curve gives
$\ln 4 / \ln 3 \approx 1.26$ -- denser than any curve of finite
length, yet far from filling the plane. Modeling the sponge by the
Menger sponge gives $\ln 20 / \ln 3 \approx 2.73$ -- more than a
surface, not quite a solid. These are exactly the more precise
comparisons of ``size'' that the introduction asked for.

While physical examples like a coastline or the surface of a sponge do 
not really have
complexity on an infinite scale, we idealize them as such with
fractals. Just as ``dimensionless'' points are an idealization useful
to describe space, fractals are also a useful idealization. 
As with all such mathematical abstractions, they
allow us to view and compute what they model in a simpler way.

\subsection{Appendix A: Examples of Self-Similar Fractals \hfill}
We briefly describe two {\it self-similar\/} fractal sets. 
The first set lives in 1 dimension, but its fractal dimension is between $0$ and $1$.
The set is called the Cantor set, named after the mathematician Georg Cantor.
This set is constructed by defining intermediate sets $C_0, C_1, \ldots$
and setting the Cantor set, $C$, to be the intersection of these sets:
$$
\eqalign{
    C &= \bigcap_{n=0}^\infty\limits C_n
}
$$
The intermediate sets have the property that $\forall n, C_{n+1} \subseteq C_n$.
These sets are defined by:
$$
\eqalign{
    C_0 &= [0, 1] \cr
    C_{n+1} &=  \hbox{For each contiguous segment of the set $C_n$, remove the open interval that is its middle third.} \cr
}
$$

As the sets, $C_n$, are nested, each successive set is an improved approximation to the Cantor set.
