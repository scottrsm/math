\section{Overview}
One of the fundamental pieces of linear algebra is a bilinear function
called the ``dot product''. It is an important workhorse, but where does
it come from? Please don't give me a definition and tell me how to compute it.
I would like to know how it came to be; or, at least, how might one
have thought of it. The rest of this paper tries to do just that.

\section{Vectors, Distance Relationships, and Projections}
Let's start with the real line and solve a simple problem. 
If a car starts at 
point $p_0$ on the real line and moves at a velocity of $v$ when will the car 
reach the point $p_1$? We assume that this problem is non-trivial:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $v \ne 0$.
	\item $p_0 \ne p_1$.
\end{itemize}

That is, find a $t^*$ so that
$$ p_0 + t^* v = p_1$$
So $t^*$ satisfies:
$$ t^* \, v = p_1 - p_0$$

Since $v$ is a (non-zero) number we can divide and solve for $t^*$:
\be
	t^* & = & \frac{p_1 - p_0}{v}
\ee

Physically, we need $t^* \ge 0$: if $t^* < 0$, the car is moving
away from $p_1$ and cannot reach it for any non-negative time.

We note, however, in this simple example that there is an answer to the question:
``What is the closest point to $p_1$ that the car can get to?''
The answer is $p_1$ when the car moves towards $p_1$ at a non-zero velocity; otherwise,
it is $p_0$.

We now look at the same problem in 2-dimensions. Points in two dimensions 
are described by a point in a plane which can be represented as a pair of 
two real numbers, $(x, y)$. More generally, one can describe a point in 3-dimensions
by a triple of numbers, $(x, y, z)$. Our plan is to try and examine the ``car''
problem in higher dimensions and look for a pattern. We will even look beyond
three dimensions!

Let's try to mimic the ``car'' argument we had in 1-dimension. But we first 
note that to mimic it more consistently, we should have some way of adding/subtracting
points (tuples) and the ability to divide by {\em numbers\/}.

We do this by replacing our ``tuple'' notation of $(x,y)$ with the {\em vector\/}
notation: $[x, y]$. Whether using tuple or vector notation, both
represent an ordered set of numbers. We will use the vector notation
because that is what mathematicians use. 

We also want to simplify the number of names we use; this is especially useful
when looking at dimensions beyond 3. We will use bold font, ${\bf x}$, to mean 
a vector (for now in 2 dimensions) whose components are: $x_1, x_2$.
But to mimic the 1-dimensional argument
we need to know how to add/subtract vectors and how to ``stretch'' a vector with
multiplication by a number. We will use the name that mathematicians use for numbers -- {\em scalar\/}.
But this is something we learn in high school -- we simply add/subtract the components 
and for multiplication by numbers we just multiply each component separately.

We generalize this from 2 or 3 dimensions so that vectors may represent $n$ dimensions.
All this means is that a vector is an ordered set of numbers of a specific length.
Here is the formal definition of how to add/subtract them and multiply them by scalars.

{\bf Definition.} Let ${\bf x}, {\bf y}$ be vectors in $n$ dimensions
(${\bf x} = [x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, ${\bf y} = [y_1, \ldots, y_n]$) and let
$a$ be a scalar. We define vector addition and scalar multiplication
component-wise:
\be
	{\bf x} + {\bf y} & \equiv & [x_1 + y_1,\, x_2 + y_2,\, \ldots,\, x_n + y_n] \\
	a * {\bf x}        & \equiv & [a x_1,\, a x_2,\, \ldots,\, a x_n]
\ee
We drop the `$*$' operator when multiplying a vector by a scalar -- just as
we drop the multiplication symbol between two scalars.


Keeping with the ``car'' example, a more 
realistic problem would be to drive on a surface -- in two dimensions -- and 
solve the problem above. To do so, we need a way to multiply a 
number (time), $t$, with a velocity vector, representing both a speed and 
a direction. And we need to be able to add this to the original position to 
get the current position. This is just what the above definitions of 
scalar multiplication and vector addition provide.
Over time, $t$, the path swept out, or {\em spanned\/} by the car is a 
line in two dimensional space: 
$L = \{{\bf p} + t \, {\bf v} \, | \, t \in {\bf R}\}$. Here, $L$ represents
a collection of ``position'' vectors representing the car's trajectory.
Check that the {\em units\/} make sense.

These definitions make sense in that the new position of a ``car'' after time 
$t$ when starting at position ${\bf p}$ is determined by taking each of the 
component positions and adjusting them by adding $t$ times the
corresponding velocity component.

Let's repeat the problem we worked out in the scalar case:
At what time, if any, will the car reach the point ${\bf p}_1$ starting at ${\bf p}_0$?
So, we need to solve for some $t^*$ so that: 
$${\bf p}_0 + t^* {\bf v} = {\bf p}_1$$
We see that $t^*$ must satisfy
\be 
	t^*{\bf v} & = & {\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0 \label{car_2_shift}
\ee
But now, we can't just divide by ${\bf v}$ to get the answer, $t^*$, 
as ${\bf v}$ is not a number.

We can think of \eqref{car_2_shift} as a shifted version of the
original problem. Instead of starting at a point, ${\bf p}_0$, we are starting at
the origin ${\bf 0}$ and trying to ``hit'' ${\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0$ using the
directional speed, ${\bf v}$.

In any event, if ${\bf v}$ is not pointing {\em exactly\/} to the destination, 
${\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0$, there is no chance of finding a solution.

Here's a different question: Drive a car on one of the roads passing through 
${\bf p}_0$ and drop someone off on this road at the point closest to the  
point ${\bf p}_1$. 

In more mathematical terms: Starting at ${\bf p}_0$, drive with velocity 
${\bf v}$ (go along some road at a fixed speed) and find the time $t^*$ at 
which I can drop off a passenger so that I am as close as possible to the 
destination, ${\bf p}_1$. 

Need to solve: Find $t^*$ so that the distance between 
${\bf p}_0 + t \, {\bf v}$ and ${\bf p}_1$ is minimized.
This can be written as: find the time, $t^*$, which minimizes the length of the
vector:
${\bf p}_0 + t\, {\bf v} - {\bf p}_1 =  t \, {\bf v} - ({\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0)$.
This is the same as finding the time to get to the closest point to the 
vector ${\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0$ using the velocity vector, ${\bf v}$, 
when starting at ${\bf 0}$.

So, we look instead at the simpler problem: Starting at ${\bf 0}$ and
driving with a velocity of ${\bf v}$, find $t^*$ so as to minimize the
distance to a given vector, ${\bf p}$.
As in the one-dimensional problem we assume ${\bf v} \neq {\bf 0}$; we make
this assumption, without further comment, whenever we project onto a vector.
We will also generalize this to $n$ dimensions and {\em define\/} the 
length of a vector, ${\bf x}$, as a generalization of the distance formula 
for vectors in 2 and 3  dimensions; namely, 
length(${\bf x}$) = $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}$.%
\footnote{Minimizing the square of the distance is the same as minimizing 
the distance, and it's also easier from the perspective of calculus.}
$$ \min_{t} \sum_{i=1}^n (p_i - t v_i)^2 $$
Using calculus, the minimum distance is achieved when the derivative
at $t^*$ is $0$:
$$ -2 \sum_{i=1}^n (p_i - t^* v_i)v_i = 0$$
$$t^* = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n p_i v_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2}$$
(This critical point is indeed a minimum: as a function of $t$ the objective
is a parabola opening upward, since the coefficient of $t^2$ is
$\sum_i v_i^2 > 0$.)
The point that is closest along the line {\em spanned\/} by ${\bf v}$ is
called the projection of ${\bf p}$ onto ${\bf v}$.
It is denoted by $P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) = t^* {\bf v}$, which is:
$$ P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n p_i v_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2} \, {\bf v} $$
We introduce a function that is a refactoring\footnote{This is a common 
practice in the software engineering world. If we start repeating code we 
refactor it into a subroutine.} of the essential element of the formula  -- as 
we see that we are repeating a sum in the numerator and the denominator.

Define the ``Dot Product'' between two vectors as: 

{\bf Definition.} The Dot Product of two vectors is defined by
$${\bf a} \cdot {\bf b} = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i.$$
Now the numerator and denominator in the fraction of the projection 
formula can be written:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \sum_{i=1}^n p_i v_i & = & {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} \\
  \sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2 & = & {\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}
\end{eqnarray*}
With this refactoring, the projection formula can be simplified to:
\be
  P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) & = & \frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{{\bf v}
  \cdot {\bf v}} {\bf v}
\ee

We use the function, $\| \cdot \|$ to denote length of a vector:
$$ \|{\bf x}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2$$
But this can be written more succinctly in terms of the dot product: 
$$ \|{\bf x}\|^2 = {\bf x} \cdot {\bf x} $$


Getting back to the original problem, the closest point to ${\bf p}_1$ when
starting at ${\bf p}_0$ and moving along ${\bf v}$ is:
$$ {\bf p}_0 + P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0)
   = {\bf p}_0 + \frac{({\bf p}_1 - {\bf p}_0)
    \cdot{\bf v}}{{\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}} {\bf v}$$

There is good reason to have ``product'' in the name ``Dot Product''.
It has most of the properties of a multiplication operator.

``Multiplicative properties'' of the Dot Product:
\bi
  \item ${\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} = {\bf v} 
      \cdot {\bf p} $ -- Dot product is symmetric.
  \item $\left(\lambda_1 {\bf p}_1 + \lambda_2 {\bf p}_2\right) 
      \cdot {\bf v} = \lambda_1 {\bf p}_1 \cdot {\bf v} + 
      \lambda_2 {\bf p}_2 \cdot {\bf v} $ 
    -- Dot product distributes addition and scalar multiplication on the left. 
    And since this operator is symmetric, it distributes on the right as well.
  \item $\|{\bf p}\| = \sqrt{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf p}}$ --
    The dot product {\em induces\/} the length function.
    Notice that the length of a vector ${\bf p}$ is positive if and only
    if ${\bf p} \neq {\bf 0}$ (and zero when ${\bf p} = {\bf 0}$).
\ei

This means that we can redo the minimization problem that gave us a
projection because the dot product distributes over vector addition and
scalar multiplication, just as ordinary multiplication does, and the
derivation proceeds using only those {\em multiplicative properties\/}.

\be
  \min_t \| {\bf p} - t \, {\bf v} \|^2 & & \nonumber \\
  \min_t ({\bf p} - t \, {\bf v}) \cdot ({\bf p} - t \, {\bf v}) & & \nonumber \\
  \min_t {\bf p} \cdot  {\bf p} - 2 t \, {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} + t^2 {\bf v} \cdot {\bf v} & & 
\ee

Notice what has happened: the vector structure has been absorbed into the
three {\em scalar\/} constants ${\bf p} \cdot {\bf p}$,
${\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}$, and ${\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}$, so the objective is a
quadratic in the single variable $t$ -- we only need single variable
calculus. (The additive constant ${\bf p} \cdot {\bf p}$ has no effect on
{\em where\/} the minimum occurs; the other two constants are what
determine it.)

Taking the derivative in $t$ and 
solving for $t$ when the derivative is 0 gives 
the minimal $t$ which we will call $t^*$. The derivative is:
\be
    -2 {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} + 2 t {\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}  & = & 0 \nonumber  \\
      t^* & = & \frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{{\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}}
\ee
As before, this critical point is a minimum, since the coefficient of
$t^2$, namely ${\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}$, is positive.

We will revisit the dot product later in this section. But for now, we will 
get back to the projection.

One way of thinking of ${\bf p}$ is as a combination of the ${\bf v}$ direction 
and another direction. We could think of the component of ${\bf p}$ in 
the ${\bf v}$ direction as 
the projection of ${\bf p}$ onto ${\bf v}$ as that is the best we can do 
using ${\bf v}$ alone to get to ${\bf p}$.
Notice that the projection of ${\bf p}$ onto ${\bf v}$ is independent of 
the ``length'' of ${\bf v}$.
If we replace ${\bf v}$ with any {\em nonzero\/} multiple of ${\bf v}$, say
$\lambda {\bf v}$ with $\lambda \neq 0$,
and look at the projection of
${\bf p}$ onto $\lambda {\bf v}$ we have:
$$ P_{\lambda {\bf v}}({\bf p}) = \frac{{\bf p} \cdot \lambda {\bf v}}{\|\lambda {\bf v}\|^2} \lambda {\bf v} =
\frac{\lambda \, {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{\lambda^2 \|{\bf v}\|^2} \lambda {\bf v}
= \frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{\|{\bf v}\|^2} {\bf v} = P_{{\bf v}}({\bf p})$$
This says that the projection of ${\bf p}$ onto ${\bf v}$ is independent of 
the length of the vector ${\bf v}$. That is, it only depends on its ``direction''.
(It doesn't matter how fast you drive me in a given direction, the place 
you drop me off to be as close to ${\bf p}$ is going to be the same.)

What about the projection of a multiple of ${\bf p}$ onto ${\bf v}$, or
combinations of ${\bf p}$ with other vectors onto ${\bf v}$?

Using the ``multiplicative properties'' of the dot product, 
it is not hard to show the following: 
\bi
  \item $P_{\bf v}(P_{\bf v}({\bf p})) = P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$.
  \item $P_{\bf v}(\lambda {\bf p}) = \lambda P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$.
  \item $P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_1 + {\bf p}_2) = P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_1) + P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_2)$.
\ei

We can combine these three into just two properties:
\bi
  \item  $P_{\bf v}(P_{\bf v}({\bf p})) = P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$ -- Makes sense: 
      We shouldn't be able to get closer doing a second projection.
\item  $P_{\bf v}(\lambda_1 {\bf p}_1 + \lambda_2 {\bf p}_2) =
    \lambda_1 P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_1) + \lambda_2 P_{\bf v}({\bf p}_2)$ --
    Powerful: Can compute projections of expressions easily based on components. 
\ei

We can think of a vector as having a direction and a length.
How do we say this precisely? Well, we can think of the direction
of ${\bf v}$ as the property shared by all vectors {\em like\/} ${\bf v}$.
All vectors like ${\bf v}$ are really just positive multiples of ${\bf v}$:
$$ \{ t \, {\bf v} \mid t > 0 \} $$
(The full set of scalar multiples, $\{ t \, {\bf v} \mid t \in {\bf R} \}$,
is the {\em line\/} through ${\bf v}$; it carries {\em two\/} directions,
one for each sign of $t$.)
In other words, vectors are like ${\bf v}$ if they have the same direction
but the ``speeds'' may vary.
(On the highway we are all going in the same direction, but with 
potentially differing speeds.)


One way to talk about the direction of this collection is to 
pick a {\em canonical\/} representative. The simplest thing to do is to 
pick the one in the same direction (orientation -- it is a positive 
multiple of ${\bf v}$) with unit length.
If ${\bf u}$ is this vector, then the projection onto the direction ${\bf v}$ is:
$$ P_{{\bf u}}({\bf p}) = \left({\bf p} \cdot {\bf u}\right) {\bf u}$$
If we continue to think of $P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$ as the component in the 
${\bf v}$ direction, then what is the other component that we add to this 
to get ${\bf p}$?
$$ {\bf p} = P_{{\bf v}}({\bf p}) + {\bf w} $$
By subtraction the other component is:
$$ {\bf w} = {\bf p} - P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) $$
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the picture to keep in mind.

Does ${\bf w}$ have a component in the ${\bf v}$ direction or vice versa?
The component of ${\bf w}$ onto ${\bf v}$ is:
$$ P_{\bf v}({\bf w}) = P_{\bf v} \left({\bf p} - P_{\bf v}({\bf p})\right) =
    P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) - P_{\bf v}(P_{\bf v}({\bf p})) = P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) - P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) = 0 $$
This computation is the algebraic form of the right angle in
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}: the leftover ${\bf w}$ has no component along
${\bf v}$.

By symmetry of the dot product, ${\bf v} \cdot {\bf w}
= {\bf w} \cdot {\bf v} = 0$; so, provided ${\bf w} \neq {\bf 0}$ (that is,
provided ${\bf p}$ is not itself a multiple of ${\bf v}$),
$P_{\bf w}({\bf v}) = 0$ as well: there
is no component of ${\bf v}$ along ${\bf w}$ either. The vectors
${\bf v}$ and ${\bf w}$ are as ``independent'' as possible.

We know that the length of a vector can be described in terms of the dot
product, and we now know that ${\bf p} = {\bf p}_v + {\bf w}$, where
${\bf p}_v = P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$ is the component along ${\bf v}$ and
${\bf w}$ is orthogonal to it.
What is the length of ${\bf p}$ when it is a sum
of two vectors that share no components between them?
\be
  \|{\bf p}\|^2 & = & {\bf p} \cdot {\bf p} = ({\bf p}_v + {\bf w}) \cdot ({\bf p}_v + {\bf w}) \nonumber \\
                & = & {\bf p}_v \cdot {\bf p}_v + 2 \, {\bf p}_v \cdot {\bf w} + {\bf w} \cdot {\bf w} \nonumber \\
                & = & \| {\bf p}_v \|^2 + 2 \cdot 0 + \| {\bf w} \|^2 \nonumber \\
                & = & \| {\bf p}_v \|^2 + \| {\bf w} \|^2
 \ee


Before moving on, notice that the machinery just assembled proves a famous
inequality with almost no additional work. The leftover has non-negative
length: $0 \le \|{\bf w}\|^2 = \|{\bf p} - P_{\bf v}({\bf p})\|^2$.
Expanding with the multiplicative properties -- a worthwhile exercise;
every piece is in hand -- gives
$$ 0 \; \le \; \left\|{\bf p} - \frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{{\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}}\,{\bf v}\right\|^2
 \; = \; {\bf p} \cdot {\bf p} \, - \, \frac{({\bf p} \cdot {\bf v})^2}{{\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}} $$
Multiplying through by ${\bf v} \cdot {\bf v} > 0$ and rearranging:
$$ ({\bf p} \cdot {\bf v})^2 \; \le \; ({\bf p} \cdot {\bf p})({\bf v} \cdot {\bf v});
\qquad \hbox{that is,} \qquad
|{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}| \; \le \; \|{\bf p}\| \, \|{\bf v}\| $$
This is the celebrated {\em Cauchy--Schwarz inequality\/} -- and the
derivation tells us when equality holds: exactly when $\|{\bf w}\| = 0$;
that is, when ${\bf p}$ is a multiple of ${\bf v}$ -- when the destination
lies on the road.

In two and three dimensions, the vectors ${\bf p}$, ${\bf p}_v$, and ${\bf w}$
form a right triangle (with ${\bf p}_v$ perpendicular to ${\bf w}$), and
the Pythagorean theorem and its converse make
$\|{\bf p}\|^2 = \|{\bf p}_v\|^2 + \|{\bf w}\|^2$ equivalent to
${\bf p}_v \cdot {\bf w} = 0$. In higher dimensions we have no a priori
geometric notion of perpendicularity, so we adopt the dot-product
condition as the {\em definition\/}: two vectors are {\em orthogonal\/}
if their dot product is zero. 
Given that two vectors ${\bf w}_1$ and ${\bf w}_2$ are orthogonal and
sum to ${\bf p}$, we have
a generalization of the Pythagorean formula, with 
$$ \|{\bf p}\|^2 = \|{\bf w}_1\|^2 + \|{\bf w}_2\|^2 $$
One can show using induction that if the vectors $\{{\bf w}_i\}_{i=1}^m$ are 
pairwise orthogonal and sum to ${\bf p}$, then
\be
  \sum_{i=1}^m {\bf w}_i & = & {\bf p} \nonumber \\
  {\bf w}_i \cdot {\bf w}_j  & = & 0 \quad \forall i, j \in [1, m] \; i \ne j \nonumber \\
  \|{\bf p}\|^2 & = & \sum_{i=1}^m \|{\bf w}_i\|^2 
\ee


\section{Standard Unit Vectors and their Generalization}
The standard unit vectors are the so-called coordinate vectors. 
In ${\bf R}^2$ these are: ${\bf e}_1 = [1, 0]$ and ${\bf e}_2 = [0, 1]$.
In ${\bf R}^n$ they are: $\{{\bf e}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ where
${\bf e}_i = [0, \ldots, 0, 1, 0, \ldots, 0]$ with the $1$ in position $i$.
Notice that the vectors in this collection have unit length and are pair-wise
orthogonal. And it is easy to write down the combination of ${\bf e}_i$ that 
produce a given ${\bf x} \in {\bf R}^n$.
$$ {\bf x} = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i {\bf e}_i $$

We claim that given another set of vectors with the same properties it is 
just as easy to write down the combination of these vectors which 
produces ${\bf x}$. Starting with ${\bf R}^2$, given two vectors 
${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$ with each of unit length and orthogonal to one 
another, assuming that we can write any ${\bf x}$
as some combination of the two, we claim that the values of $u_1$ and $u_2$ 
such that ${\bf x} = u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2$ are easy to compute.

So, given that ${\bf x}$ can be written as a combination of two we have:
\be
   {\bf x} & = & u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2
\ee
From this we can form two equations, each the dot product with 
${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$ respectively:
\be
  {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_1 & = & u_{1} {\bf v}_1 \cdot {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_1 \cdot {\bf v}_2 \\
  {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_2 & = & u_{1} {\bf v}_2 \cdot {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \cdot {\bf v}_2
\ee
But since the ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$ have unit length, the dot product 
with themselves is 1; and since the dot product of one with another is 0, we 
may write these two equations as:
\be
  {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_1 & = & u_1 \\
  {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_2 & = & u_2
\ee
That is, we can instantly solve what would be a system of equations --
which would get progressively harder in higher dimensions.
We can write this more formally as:

{\bf Theorem.} If ${\bf x} \in {\bf R}^2$ is a combination of unit-length
orthogonal vectors ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$, then that combination is:
$$ {\bf x} = ({\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_1 ) {\bf v}_1 + ({\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_2 ) {\bf v}_2 $$
 

If it is the case that in ${\bf R}^n$ we have $n$ vectors of unit length and which 
are all orthogonal to each other, then by the same reasoning 
(assuming that these $n$ vectors can span the full space) we have:%
\footnote{It turns out, as you might imagine, that $n$ pairwise-orthogonal
{\em nonzero\/} vectors (each pointing in a ``different direction'') are
always able to span ${\bf R}^n$ -- and unit-length vectors are in
particular nonzero. Half of this is one line with the trick used above:
dotting a vanishing combination $\sum_i c_i {\bf v}_i = {\bf 0}$ with
${\bf v}_j$ gives $c_j = 0$, so the vectors are independent; and $n$
independent vectors in an $n$-dimensional space must span it.}

{\bf Theorem.} If ${\bf x}$ is a vector in ${\bf R}^n$, and
$\{{\bf v}_i\}_{i=1}^n$ are vectors of unit length pairwise orthogonal
to each other, then ${\bf x}$ may be written as:
\be
{\bf x} & = & \sum_{i=1}^n ({\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_i ) {\bf v}_i  \label{orthonorm_expansion} 
\ee

We give a name to collections of such orthogonal vectors.

{\bf Definition.} An {\em orthonormal\/} set is a collection of $m$ vectors
$\{ {\bf v}_i \}_{i=1}^m$, each of unit length, that are pairwise orthogonal.

\section{Projection onto a Linear Space}

We can repeat what we did in the last section, considering instead that we 
can travel on an incline representing a mountain in 3-space. We are free to 
drive anywhere on the 
side of the mountain and want to drop off someone so that they can be 
picked up by a helicopter based on an adjacent mountain. 
We want to drop the person off so that
the helicopter flies the least distance -- 
helicopter time and fuel are expensive.

We can proceed in the same way as before, but now rather than a single 
direction to drive in we have what amounts to two directions. 
We are saying that there is a direction ${\bf v}_1$ 
and a direction ${\bf v}_2$ so that we can get to any point on the mountain 
if we drive for some time in the direction ${\bf v}_1$ and then drive in
the direction ${\bf v}_2$.

The solution to the problem is similar to the last section:
\be
    \min_{u_1,u_2} \| {\bf p} - \left( u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \right)\|^2
\ee
Just as before, we solve to find $u_1^*$, $u_2^*$ that minimize the above. 
This occurs when the {\em partial\/} derivatives of $u_1$ and $u_2$ are $0$.
Since the distance can be written in terms of the dot product we may 
rewrite this minimization problem as:%
\footnote{We are assuming that the mountain is represented by a plane that 
goes through the origin.}

\be
  \min_{u_1,u_2} \left( {\bf p} - \left( u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \right) \right) \cdot \left( {\bf p} - \left( u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \right) \right) & & \nonumber \\
\ee
Taking the derivatives of this with respect to $u_1$ and $u_2$ we have:%
\footnote{This follows since the dot product behaves like multiplication
and the derivative of
$f(x) \cdot f(x)$ is $f'(x) \cdot f(x) + f(x) \cdot f'(x) = 2 f'(x) \cdot f(x)$.
By the same reasoning, for $g(t) = f(t, {\bf a}) \cdot f(t, {\bf a})$,
$g'(t) = 2 f'(t, {\bf a}) \cdot f(t, {\bf a})$.}

\be
  -2 {\bf v}_1 \cdot \left({\bf p} - \left( u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \right) \right) & = & 0 \\
  -2 {\bf v}_2 \cdot \left({\bf p} -  \left( u_1 {\bf v}_1 + u_2 {\bf v}_2 \right) \right) & = & 0 
\ee
These two equations are the {\em normal equations\/} -- the reader who has
met ``least squares'' has seen them before. They are solvable for any two
independent directions, but the system couples $u_1$ and $u_2$.
If we use orthonormal vectors ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$,
the coupling vanishes and this simplifies greatly to:

\be
  u_1 & = & {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}_1 \\
  u_2 & = & {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}_2
\ee
(This critical point is again the minimum: with orthonormal
${\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2$ the objective expands to
$(u_1 - {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}_1)^2 + (u_2 - {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}_2)^2$
plus a constant.)

Nor is orthonormality a real restriction. Given any two independent
directions, keep the first; subtract from the second its projection onto
the first -- the leftover construction ${\bf w} = {\bf p} - P_{\bf v}({\bf p})$
yet again -- and scale both to unit length. Iterating this recipe on any
spanning collection is called the {\em Gram--Schmidt process\/}: it
manufactures an orthonormal set out of whatever directions you were
handed.

If we use the notation $\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2\}$ to indicate
the set of points {\em spanned\/} (swept out) by ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$,
then when ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$ are orthonormal we have:
\be
  P_{\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2\}}({\bf x}) & = & ( {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_1 ) {\bf v}_1 +  ( {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_2 ) {\bf v}_2  \label{proj2}
\ee
Figure~\ref{fig:plane} shows the geometry.  It is the two-direction
analogue of Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}: the projection lies in the plane
spanned by ${\bf v}_1$ and ${\bf v}_2$, and the leftover
${\bf w} = {\bf p} - P_{\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, {\bf v}_2\}}({\bf p})$
meets the {\em entire\/} plane at a right angle -- it is orthogonal to
${\bf v}_1$, to ${\bf v}_2$, and hence to every combination of them.

{\bf Definition.} We define $P_{\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, \ldots, {\bf v}_m\}}$ to be
the projection onto the set of points {\em spanned\/} (swept out) by the
vectors ${\bf v}_1, \ldots, {\bf v}_m$.

Formula \eqref{proj2} generalizes: if
$\{{\bf v}_i\}_{i=1}^m$ is an orthonormal collection of vectors, then the
projection of a given vector ${\bf x} \in {\bf R}^n$ onto the
space spanned by the collection is given by:
\be
  P_{\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, \ldots, {\bf v}_m\}}({\bf x}) & = & \sum_{i=1}^m ( {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_i) {\bf v}_i
\ee
Note that when $n = m$, we reproduce formula \eqref{orthonorm_expansion},
as $P_{\mathrm{span}\{{\bf v}_1, \ldots, {\bf v}_n\}}({\bf x}) = {\bf x}$.

\section{Generalizing the ``Dot Product'': Inner Products}

One can repeat the above notion of projection using a distance measure created from a generalization of the dot product.
The generalization is called an {\em inner product}. We will use an ``infix'' form of such a function and use the notation
$\langle{\bf x}, {\bf y}\rangle$. An inner product is any function
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \rightarrow {\bf R}$ with the following properties:
\bi
  \item  $ \langle {\bf x}, {\bf y}\rangle \, = \, \langle{\bf y}, {\bf x}\rangle$ -- Symmetry.
  \item  $ \langle \lambda_1 {\bf x}_1 + \lambda_2 {\bf x}_2, {\bf y}\rangle \, = \, 
      \lambda_1 \langle{\bf x}_1, {\bf y}\rangle + \lambda_2 \langle{\bf x}_2, {\bf y}\rangle $ -- Distributes addition and scalar multiplication.
  \item $\langle {\bf x}, {\bf x}\rangle \ge 0$, with equality iff ${\bf x} = {\bf 0}$ -- Positive definiteness.
\ei
The inner product {\em induces\/} a norm $\|{\bf x}\| \equiv \sqrt{\langle{\bf x}, {\bf x}\rangle}$,
giving a notion of length of vectors. The induced ``length'' has the properties of a length.
\bi
  \item $\|{\bf x}\| = 0 \quad \text{iff} \quad {\bf x} = {\bf 0}$. -- Non-degeneracy.
  \item $\|\lambda {\bf x}\| = |\lambda| \|{\bf x}\|\quad \forall \lambda \in {\bf R} \quad \forall {\bf x} \in V$. -- Homogeneity.
  \item $\|{\bf x} + {\bf y} \| \le \|{\bf x}\| + \|{\bf y}\| \quad {\bf x}, {\bf y} \in V.$ -- Triangle inequality.%
\footnote{The first two properties follow directly from the inner-product
axioms; the triangle inequality requires the {\em Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality\/}, $|\langle{\bf x}, {\bf y}\rangle| \le \|{\bf x}\|\,\|{\bf y}\|$
-- which we proved for the dot product from the leftover's non-negative
length, $\|{\bf w}\|^2 \ge 0$. That two-line proof used only the
multiplicative properties, so it runs verbatim for any inner product.}
\ei

If we repeat the exercise to determine the projection of one vector onto 
another by the length induced by an inner product, then all of the 
formulas above are duplicated with the only change being the replacement 
of ${\bf x} \cdot {\bf y}$ with $\langle{\bf x}, {\bf y}\rangle$.

The length and projection formulas become:
\be
  \|{\bf p}\|^2 & = & \langle {\bf p}, {\bf p} \rangle \\
  P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) & = & \frac{\langle {\bf p}, {\bf v} \rangle}{\langle {\bf v}, {\bf v} \rangle} {\bf v}
\ee

One of the things we needed was that we could do calculus
(we needed to minimize a length in order to find the projection),
and for this we needed a notion of length. The inner product {\em defines\/} 
a notion of length automatically and all of the calculations proceed as before.
The only change is that wherever we see a dot product we can replace it with 
the inner product.

Example: We can define the following inner product on ${\bf R}^n$.
Let ${\bf w}$ be a given vector with strictly positive entries:
$w_i > 0 \quad \forall i \in [1, n]$.
Then we can define an inner product, $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ as:
$$ \langle {\bf x}, {\bf y} \rangle \equiv \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i y_i$$
(positive weights are all that is needed for symmetry, bilinearity, and
positive definiteness; the additional normalization $\sum_i w_i = 1$ is
standard when the $w_i$ are interpreted as probability weights but is
not required for the inner-product property.)
This modified ``dot product'' allows us to favor or discount some components
over others. This is often used in data science.

The generalization is powerful and we can apply the geometric intuition of 
projections to sets of objects like functions and 
produce remarkable results.
For instance, we can define a function space
\footnote{The exact notion of ``measurable'' requires further
explanation which we won't provide.  (One might expect the word
``integrable'' here, but square-integrability does not require
integrability: on $\Omega = [1, \infty)$ the function $1/x$ has
$\int f^2 < \infty$ while $\int |f| = \infty$.)}
$$L^2 = \{ {\bf f} \mid \text{$f$ is measurable with} \int_\Omega f(x)^2 \, dx < \infty\}.$$

$L^2$ is a set of functions where addition and scalar multiplication 
exist just like numeric vectors.
Addition and scalar multiplication are defined by:%
\footnote{This is like vectors where ``x'' is the continuous ``index''
into the ``vector'' (function).
And just like vectors, to define one you need to explain what the 
value of the vector is on each ``index''.}
\be
  ({\bf f} + {\bf g})(x) & \equiv & f(x) + g(x) \\
  (\lambda * {\bf f})(x) & \equiv & \lambda f(x)
\ee
To understand the above, keep in mind what one must do. You must define a ``+''
operator so that adding two functions produces another function.
But to describe what this function $f + g$ is, you must show what it 
does when applied to any ``x'' -- which we've done.
The new function $f + g$ when applied to ``x'' is what one would expect; it is
the sum of the values of $f$ and $g$ applied to $x$.
In a similar way we define scalar multiplication. And just like with 
ordinary vectors, we suppress the multiplication operator and write 
$\lambda \, {\bf f}$, instead of  $\lambda * {\bf f} $.

With these definitions this space of functions behaves like
vectors of numbers.

Define $\langle{\bf f}, {\bf g}\rangle \equiv \int_\Omega f(x) g(x) \, dx$.
This induces the length $\| {\bf f} \| = \, \sqrt{\langle {\bf f}, {\bf f} \rangle} = \sqrt{\int_\Omega f(x)^2 \, dx}$.%
\footnote{One caveat: a function that is $0$ except at a few isolated
points has $\langle {\bf f}, {\bf f} \rangle = 0$ without being the zero
function, so positive definiteness fails as literally stated.  The
standard remedy is to declare two functions to be the {\em same\/} vector
whenever they agree except on a set the integral cannot see (``almost
everywhere'').  With that identification, $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$
is an honest inner product.}

So, this generalization can apply to things that are very different 
looking from typical vectors.

Given two functions ${\bf v}$ and ${\bf p}$, the projection formula becomes:
\be
  P_{\bf v}({\bf p}) & = & \frac{\int_\Omega p(x) \, v(x) \, dx}{\int_\Omega v(x)^2 \, dx} {\bf v}
\ee
Notice the projection is a new vector (function) that is a multiple of
the function ${\bf v}$. You can evaluate it on a number just like
any other function:
$$\left(P_{\bf v}({\bf p})\right)(x) = \frac{\int_\Omega p(s) \, v(s) \, ds}{\int_\Omega v(s)^2 \, ds} \, v(x)$$

\section{The Whole Story in One Table}
The paper has been one formula surviving ever larger boards:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l}
{\bf Board} & {\bf ``Divide by ${\bf v}$''} & {\bf Formula} \\
\hline
${\bf R}^1$ & honest division & $t^* = (p_1 - p_0)/v$ \\
${\bf R}^n$, a line & divide as best you can & $t^* = {\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} \, / \, {\bf v} \cdot {\bf v}$ \\
${\bf R}^n$, a subspace & one division per direction & $u_i = {\bf x} \cdot {\bf v}_i$ \ (orthonormal) \\
inner product space & same, new product & $\langle {\bf p}, {\bf v}\rangle \, / \, \langle {\bf v}, {\bf v}\rangle$ \\
$L^2$ (functions) & same, product is an integral & $\int p\, v \, \big/ \int v^2$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

What changes at each rung is only what ``multiply and divide by
${\bf v}$'' means; the strategic move -- get as close as you can, and the
leftover is orthogonal -- never changes.

{\bf Companion papers.} This projection idea is the trunk of several
companion papers. The weighted inner product example above is not
hypothetical: in ``Properties of a Linear Unbiased Minimum Variance
Estimator'' the weights are the reciprocals $1/\sigma_i^2$ of measurement
variances, and the Gauss--Markov argument there is exactly this paper's
orthogonality-plus-Pythagoras step. In ``Measure Theoretic Conditional
Expectation in an Elementary Setting'' the projection is taken in $L^2$ --
onto the subspace of functions measurable with respect to a coarser
$\sigma$-algebra -- and the ``best guess'' of probability theory is
revealed as $P_{\bf v}$ played in function space. What makes the geometric
intuition safe in $L^2$ -- the {\em completeness\/} that guarantees
nearest points exist -- is the subject of ``Why Lebesgue Integration''.
And in ``Singular Transformations of Probability Density Functions'' the
minimum-norm pseudo inverse is this same best-approximation construction,
run from the other side.

\section{Coda: the Angle}
There is one more classical piece of the story.  The Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality -- proved earlier from the leftover's non-negative length --
says that for nonzero vectors,
$$ -1 \; \le \; \frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{\|{\bf p}\| \, \|{\bf v}\|}
\; \le \; 1. $$
In two and three dimensions this ratio is exactly the cosine of the angle
between ${\bf p}$ and ${\bf v}$ (compare the projection formula with the
usual trigonometry of a right triangle).  In higher dimensions -- where we
have no protractors -- we turn this around and use it as the
{\em definition\/} of the angle $\theta$ between two nonzero vectors:
$$ \cos \theta \; \equiv \;
\frac{{\bf p} \cdot {\bf v}}{\|{\bf p}\| \, \|{\bf v}\|}. $$
This is the same move we made with orthogonality: a theorem in low
dimensions is adopted as a definition in general.  With it, the dot
product acquires its familiar geometric reading,
${\bf p} \cdot {\bf v} = \|{\bf p}\| \, \|{\bf v}\| \cos \theta$: it
measures {\em how aligned\/} two vectors are, scaled by their lengths --
which is exactly the role it played in the projection formula where it
was born. And the equality case of Cauchy--Schwarz gives the angle its
extremes: $\cos\theta = \pm 1$ exactly when the leftover ${\bf w}$
vanishes -- when the destination lies on the road.

{\bf Further reading.} Any linear algebra text develops these ideas in
full; see, for example, G. Strang, {\it Introduction to Linear
Algebra\/}, or S. Axler, {\it Linear Algebra Done Right\/}.
